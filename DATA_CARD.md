# ITIEC — data card

**ITIEC** (Indonesian Text-in-Image Error Correction) has two parts: a manually
annotated real corpus used for domain adaptation and all reported evaluation, and a
synthetic corpus used for pre-training and development.

---

## ITIEC-Real

**653 images, 804 annotated sentences**, collected from public Indonesian sources:
street signage, social-media screenshots, and product labels.

### Annotators and procedure

The corpus was labelled by two annotators reading for a degree in Indonesian language
education at Universitas Siliwangi, coordinated by a member of that department's
faculty. The six categories and the boundaries between them were fixed in a written
guideline before annotation began. Each annotator labelled a disjoint subset of the
images (333 and 320 images); unclear cases were settled by discussion against the
guideline. In a second pass, 94 images were labelled independently by both annotators:
category-level agreement on that subset is **Cohen's κ = 0.63** over the seven-way
decision, with disagreement concentrated on adjacent categories (spelling vs.
typographic, algospeak vs. spelling, morphological vs. syntactic). In the released
file, annotators are pseudonymised as `A1` and `A2`.

### Annotation schema

Per sentence region:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `bbox_normalized` | verified region of the sentence in the image (x, y, w, h in [0, 1]) |
| `teks_ocr` | faithful transcription of the text **as written**, errors preserved |
| `kalimat_koreksi` | the corrected standard-Indonesian sentence |
| `errors[]` | list of annotated errors; each has `token_salah`, `token_benar`, `category` |

The corpus holds **3,267 error annotations**: 1,430 spelling, 701 typographic,
627 algospeak, 213 morphological, 189 semantic, 76 syntactic, 31 mixed. Spans are
usually single tokens; a longer span is marked where the correction cannot be
decomposed.

Note the naming: `teks_ocr` is the *human* transcription (the oracle input), not OCR
output. OCR hypotheses live in `paddleocr_out` in `results/e2e_ocr_outputs.csv`.
Conflating the two is the single easiest way to misread Tab. ladder.

Splits are **by image**, stratified to preserve category balance — no image contributes
sentences to more than one split.

The distribution is what real Indonesian visual text actually looks like, not what a
balanced benchmark would prefer: spelling and typographic errors dominate, algospeak is
a substantial minority (19%), and SEM/MOR/SIN together are under 6%. Those three are
genuinely low-resource and are reported separately throughout the paper rather than
folded into a headline average. With a test count of 1 (SIN) and 4 (MOR), per-category
numbers for them are illustrative only.

### Evaluation set

Test regions are cropped by their verified bounding box. After removing three sentences
with an empty OCR output or empty reference, **201 of 204** test sentences form the
evaluation set used in every real-image experiment in the paper. The domain-adaptation
training set is the 526 real training sentences (source = transcription, target =
correction).

### Limitations a reader should weigh

- N = 201 for the test set. The paper's significant results (45 improvements, 0
  regressions) survive this; the non-significant ones (39 vs 38) are exactly what a
  sample this size cannot resolve, and are reported as such.
- Single reference per sentence. This understates free-form correctors — GPT-4o is
  occasionally *more* correct than the annotation (restoring `viral` where the
  annotator left the truncation `vira`). A multi-reference or human evaluation would
  give a fairer absolute ranking; this is stated as future work rather than quietly
  omitted.
- Agreement (κ = 0.63) is moderate, in line with error-type annotation in GEC; the
  orthographic categories do not separate cleanly, which is a property of the task.

## ITIEC-Syn

Generated by applying controlled corruption — algospeak substitution, typographic
noise, spelling perturbation — drawn from a 520-rule Indonesian algospeak/informal
dictionary, to clean sentences held out from the IGED GEC corpus.

| Split | Pairs |
|---|---|
| train | 46,384 |
| validation | 2,441 |
| test (held out) | 2,500 |

The **test split is contamination-free by construction**: it is built with the same
corruption recipe and category proportions, from IGED sentences whose corrected form
occurs in neither the training nor the validation split. The validation split is used
*only* for early stopping. All synthetic correction results in the paper are on the
test split. Generator: `synthetic/generate_synthetic.py`; test-split builder:
`src/make_syn_test.py` (which copies the injection functions verbatim so the two cannot
drift apart).

Category labels are attached as source-side tags during generation and removed at
tokenisation, so the corrector operates on plain text at inference.

## Knowledge resources

| Resource | File | Size | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Variant mapping table | `resources/algospeak_dict.json` | ~11 KB | 520 algospeak/abbreviation rules |
| Validity lexicon | derived from the corpus dictionary | — | gates every rewrite; **the binding resource** (Fig. lexcurve) |
| Semantic knowledge graph | `semantic_kg.json` | 76 MB | CASTLE training-time signal; distributed separately (exceeds GitHub's limit) |

The paper's Fig. lexcurve result is that accuracy is flat in the mapping table and rises almost
linearly with lexicon coverage without saturating. The handcrafted table turns out to be
redundant on this distribution — all 120 tokens it matches are already in the validity
lexicon, so the guard blocks the rewrite. Inspection shows the guard is protective:
many blocked entries are paraphrase-like (`ada→terdapat`, `viral→hits`) and would
corrupt valid text if applied.

## Ethics and licence

Compiled from publicly available Indonesian sources. Only text regions relevant to the
correction task were retained: **no personal account identifiers, user metadata, or
faces** were collected or are released.

The corpus contains moderation-evading *algospeak*, including terms referring to
sensitive topics. These are included solely as linguistic objects of study — that is
precisely the phenomenon the normaliser addresses — and do not reflect the views of the
authors.
